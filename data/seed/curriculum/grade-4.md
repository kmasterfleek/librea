# Grade 4: Everything Has Shape

**Theme**: Abstraction and Representation
**Core Capacity**: Creating and reading abstract encodings that require embodied context to decode

---

## Philosophy

Fourth grade is the year children learn that *the same thing can be represented in different ways, and each representation reveals different truths*. The Marshall Islands stick chart tradition takes center stage: abstract maps that are useless without the body. Children build abstractions, test whether others can decode them, and discover that every map, chart, and symbol is a **lossy compression** of reality. What you encode is a choice. What you leave out is a choice. Both choices matter.

## Key Practices

### Stick Charts
Children build abstract representations using sticks, string, and objects. These encode real spatial or social patterns — but like Marshall Islands charts, they require being *in the space* to read. A stick chart of the playground's social dynamics makes no sense at a desk. You must walk the playground to decode it.

### The 5-Number Description
The pivotal exercise of the year. Teacher holds up an apple. "Describe this apple using exactly 5 numbers." Children propose: weight, redness (1-10), size, number of bruises, sweetness guess. Then another apple. Same 5 measurements. "Which apple is more similar to a third apple?"

This is **vector comparison** in plain language. By spring, children describe classmates, weather days, soil samples, and garden plots using number-lists. They begin to intuit that *similar number-lists mean similar things*.

### Lossy Compression
Every encoding exercise ends with: "What did your representation capture? What did it leave out?" Children learn that every chart, every map, every description is incomplete. The question is never "is this perfect?" but "is this useful enough for this purpose?" This is the foundation of dimensional thinking — choosing which dimensions matter for a given question.

## Observable Growth

By June, a fourth grader can:

- Build an abstract stick chart encoding a real spatial or social pattern
- Decode someone else's abstraction with >60% accuracy
- Describe any object or phenomenon using a consistent list of 5+ measurements
- Articulate what their encoding captured and what it missed
- Compare two things by comparing their number-lists
- Conduct a multi-week investigation and present findings using multiple representations

## The Key Insight for This Year

When a child says "This soil sample is [dark, 3, wet, 7 worms, acidic] and that one is [light, 1, dry, 2 worms, neutral]" — they have just created **two 5-dimensional vectors** and are comparing them by inspection. When they say "the first one is more like the compost bin sample" — they have just performed **nearest-neighbor search** by intuition.

They don't know the math yet. They don't need to. The cognitive structure is in place: *things can be described by lists of measurements, and similar lists mean similar things*. Everything that follows — vector spaces, cosine similarity, HNSW graphs — is formalization of what they already feel in their hands.
