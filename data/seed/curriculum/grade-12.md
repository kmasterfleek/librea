# Grade 12: Everything You Build

**Theme**: Agency and Legacy
**Core Capacity**: Creating something sovereign — a system, tool, investigation, or institution — that outlasts the student and serves others

---

## Philosophy

Twelfth grade is the year the student becomes the author. For twelve years, they have been apprentices to a way of seeing: the world is high-dimensional, meaning lives in the relationships between dimensions, data is powerful and political, and understanding requires both rigor and humility. Now they demonstrate what they've become by **building something that matters**.

Every senior completes a **capstone project**: a substantial, original work that uses the full stack of Omega skills to address a real problem. Some build tools. Some conduct research. Some create curricula. Some launch community initiatives. The project is not an assignment. It is a declaration: *this is what I can do, this is what I care about, and this is what I will leave behind*.

The year also includes formal mentorship of younger students, a public capstone defense, and a reflective portfolio in which each student articulates their thirteen-year journey from tying knots on string to building sovereign systems.

## Key Practices

### The Capstone Project
The defining work of the Omega education. Requirements:
1. **Real problem**: addresses a genuine need in the school, community, or broader world
2. **Vector thinking**: uses dimensional/vector concepts in a substantive way (not superficially)
3. **Technical depth**: involves building something — a tool, system, dataset, curriculum, or analysis pipeline
4. **Ethical consideration**: includes explicit analysis of who benefits, who might be harmed, and how the student has addressed this
5. **Public defense**: presented to a panel of teachers, community members, and at least one external expert

Example capstone projects:
- A local air quality monitoring network using ESP32 sensors, with vector-based anomaly detection and a public dashboard
- A multilingual book recommendation system for the school library that works entirely locally
- A curriculum module for Grade 4 that teaches correlation vs. causation through garden data
- An investigation of housing stability patterns in the school district using public data, with policy recommendations presented to the school board
- A privacy-preserving student matching system that helps new students find compatible study partners without revealing personal data

### The Mentorship Commitment
Every senior is paired with 2-3 younger students for the full year. This is not tutoring. It is mentorship — relationship, guidance, and modeling. Seniors help their mentees navigate challenges, set goals, and see how the long Omega arc connects. The mentorship relationship is the primary mechanism by which the school's culture transmits across generations.

### The Journey Portfolio
Each senior assembles a portfolio that traces their development from kindergarten through twelfth grade. It includes:
- **Artifacts**: selected quipus, garden vectors, stick charts, research papers, project outputs
- **Narrative**: a reflective essay articulating how they think differently than they would have in a traditional school, and what they're taking forward
- **Self-vector**: a final "human vector" — the student describes themselves in as many dimensions as they choose, with annotations explaining why each dimension matters

The portfolio is not graded. It is **witnessed** — read and responded to by teachers, mentors, and peers.

### Situation Room Stewardship
Seniors are responsible for the Situation Room's operation. They maintain data pipelines, design new visualizations, train younger students to interpret displays, and decide what questions the school should be asking of its data. This is leadership through infrastructure — the most Omega form of service.

## Observable Growth

By June, a twelfth grader can:

- Conceive, plan, execute, and publicly defend a substantial capstone project
- Manage a long-term project with self-directed milestones, setbacks, and iteration
- Mentor younger students and articulate the Omega learning progression from experience
- Operate and maintain the Situation Room's data infrastructure
- Write a reflective portfolio that traces thirteen years of dimensional thinking development
- Articulate — in their own words, to any audience — what it means to think in vectors and why it matters
- Build something sovereign: a system that works without depending on external platforms or corporate infrastructure

## The Key Insight for This Year

When a graduating senior stands before the community and says: "In kindergarten, I tied knots on string to count ladybugs. In fourth grade, I described soil with five numbers and realized similar numbers meant similar soil. In eighth grade, I built a search engine with index cards and string. In tenth grade, I audited our school's data system and found that it encoded my introversion as a deficit. For my capstone, I built a peer matching system that treats introversion as a dimension, not a deficiency — and it runs on a $35 computer in the library closet, because I believe the school should own its own tools" — they have articulated a **thirteen-year arc from embodied cognition to sovereign technology**.

They are not a data scientist. They are not a programmer. They are not an activist. They are something the traditional system doesn't have a word for: a person who understands how the world is measured, who chose the measurements, whose interests they serve, and what to build when the measurements are wrong.

This is what Omega produces. Not test scores. Not college readiness metrics. People who see the frame and can build a better one.
