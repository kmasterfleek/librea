# Grade 10: Everything Has Ownership

**Theme**: Data Sovereignty and Ethics
**Core Capacity**: Understanding who controls data, who benefits from it, and how to build systems that serve communities rather than extract from them

---

## Philosophy

Tenth grade confronts the political reality of vectors. Data is not neutral. Every measurement system serves someone's interests. Student vectors can be used to support students or to surveil them. Sensor networks can serve communities or exploit them. Algorithms can redistribute power or concentrate it. This year, students learn the ethics, politics, and economics of data — not as abstract philosophy but as design decisions they themselves must make.

The central question: **Who owns the vector?** When a student's 15-dimensional profile sits in a database, who controls it? Who profits from it? Who can see it? Who can challenge it? These are not hypothetical questions. Students examine real systems — credit scores, social media profiles, predictive policing algorithms, educational analytics — and evaluate them through the lens of sovereignty: does this system give power to the person it describes, or take power from them?

## Key Practices

### The Data Negotiation
Students' garden sensor data has been flowing freely for years. This year, a (simulated) agricultural company wants to buy aggregated data from all school gardens. Students must negotiate: What data is shared? At what granularity? Who sees individual plot data vs. aggregates? What happens if the company sells the data to a third party? Can students revoke access?

This exercise makes abstract privacy debates concrete. Students draft actual data-sharing agreements and debate terms in class.

### The Audit
Students turn the critical lens on their own school's Situation Room. They examine the 15-dimensional student vector: Who chose these dimensions? Whose values do they encode? What populations might be systematically misrepresented? What happens when the vector is wrong? Is there a process for a student to challenge their own profile?

Students write formal audit reports with recommendations. Administration reads them and responds.

### Local-First AI
Students build AI systems that run entirely on local hardware. A plant disease detector trained on their garden photos. A book recommender using their library vectors. A study group matcher using anonymous student profiles. The constraint: **no data leaves the device** unless the user explicitly consents.

This is the technical embodiment of data sovereignty — AI that serves the user without requiring the user to surrender their data to a corporation.

### Techno-Feudalism Analysis
Students study how major platforms (social media, e-commerce, educational technology) extract value from user data. They map the economic flows: who generates the data? Who captures the value? Who bears the risk? They compare this to historical feudal systems and discuss whether the analogy holds. Students propose alternative architectures — cooperatives, commons, public infrastructure — and evaluate the tradeoffs.

## Observable Growth

By June, a tenth grader can:

- Draft a data-sharing agreement with specific terms for consent, access, and revocation
- Audit a data system for ethical issues and write a formal report with recommendations
- Build a local-first AI application that processes data without cloud dependency
- Explain the economic dynamics of platform data extraction
- Articulate three different models of data governance (corporate, commons, sovereign) with tradeoffs
- Evaluate whether a specific use of student vectors is ethical using a framework they can defend
- Propose a policy for a real data system and defend it against critique

## The Key Insight for This Year

When a student audits the school's student vector system and writes: "Dimension 13 (ELL status) is binary but multilingualism is a spectrum. Dimension 10 (trajectory) is calculated by the system but never shown to the student. Dimension 15 (peer-connectedness) encodes introversion as a deficit. These aren't neutral measurements — they're value judgments embedded in numbers" — they have understood that **vectors are political artifacts**.

Every dimension is a choice. Every weight is a value. Every threshold is a policy decision wearing the disguise of mathematics. The student who can see this — who can audit the vector, identify the embedded values, and propose alternatives — is not just data-literate. They are **data-sovereign**. They will never be a passive subject of someone else's measurement system. They will always ask: who chose these dimensions, and who benefits?
