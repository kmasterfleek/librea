# Grade 9: Everything Is Perception

**Theme**: Signal, Noise, and Meaning
**Core Capacity**: Distinguishing reliable signal from noise, understanding how perception shapes what data gets collected and what gets ignored

---

## Philosophy

Ninth grade is the year students turn the lens inward. They've spent eight years learning to measure the world, encode it as vectors, search it, and find patterns. Now the question becomes: **who decided what to measure?** Every vector is a choice. Every dimension included is a dimension someone selected. Every dimension excluded is a dimension someone ignored — or couldn't see. Perception is not passive reception. It is active construction. And the same is true for data.

This year, **perceptual literacy** becomes an explicit subject. Students study how human perception works (attention, framing, cognitive bias), how media manipulates perception (framing effects, selective data presentation, misinformation), and how algorithms create perception (recommendation loops, filter bubbles, engagement optimization). The goal is not cynicism. It is **sovereignty** — the ability to see how your perception is being shaped, and to choose how to shape it yourself.

## Key Practices

### The Dual Vector
Students maintain two simultaneous records of their garden: the ESP32 sensor vector (quantitative, continuous, objective within its measurement range) and a personal observation vector (qualitative dimensions quantified by judgment — beauty, harmony, surprise, worry). Each week they compare: where do the two agree? Where do they diverge? When your gut says the garden is struggling but the sensors say everything is fine, which is right? When the sensors flag an anomaly you didn't notice, what did you miss?

This is training in **epistemic humility** — the understanding that no single measurement system captures everything.

### Signal vs. Noise
Students receive messy datasets — sensor readings with instrument error, survey data with inconsistent responses, historical records with gaps. They learn to distinguish:
- **Signal**: the pattern that carries information
- **Noise**: random variation that obscures the pattern
- **Bias**: systematic distortion that creates a false pattern

They practice denoising techniques: averaging, outlier detection, triangulation across sources. They learn that "cleaning data" is itself a perceptual act — what you call noise and what you call signal depends on what you're looking for.

### The Framing Exercise
Teacher presents the same event described by three different news sources. Students identify: what dimensions does each source measure? What dimensions does each ignore? How does the choice of dimensions change the conclusion? Students then write their own account of a school event using deliberately different framings. The class reads them all and discusses which version is "truest" — and discovers that the question itself is more complex than it seems.

### The Missing Dimension Audit
Students select an existing dataset — a published study, a government report, a company's metrics dashboard — and identify dimensions that are **absent**. What isn't being measured? Who might be affected by the absence? Students write a formal "Missing Dimension Report" proposing what should be added and why.

## Observable Growth

By June, a ninth grader can:

- Maintain dual measurement systems (sensor + observation) and analyze their divergence
- Identify signal, noise, and bias in a real dataset
- Analyze a media source for dimensional framing and articulate what's missing
- Perform basic vector operations and explain dimensionality reduction
- Write a "Missing Dimension Report" for an existing dataset or system
- Explain how recommendation algorithms create filter bubbles using vector similarity
- Conduct independent research on a perception-related question with original data

## The Key Insight for This Year

When a student reads a news article about school performance that reports only test scores and graduation rates, and says "They're projecting a 15-dimensional student onto 2 dimensions — of course the outliers disappear, of course the resilient kids look the same as the declining kids, of course you can't see the intervention successes" — they have applied **dimensional thinking to media criticism**. They understand that every dataset, every report, every headline is a projection — a lossy compression of a higher-dimensional reality. And they know, from nine years of embodied practice, what gets lost in compression.

This is perceptual literacy. Not the ability to detect lies, but the ability to see the frame. The student who can see the frame can choose whether to accept it. That's sovereignty.
