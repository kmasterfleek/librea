# Grade 11: Everything Integrates

**Theme**: Synthesis Across Scales
**Core Capacity**: Designing and executing complex, multi-stakeholder projects that integrate technical skill, ethical reasoning, and community impact

---

## Philosophy

Eleventh grade is the integration year. Students have all the pieces: embodied pattern recognition (K-3), dimensional thinking (4-5), vector operations (6-8), perceptual literacy (9), and data sovereignty (10). Now they put it all together. The question shifts from "Can you understand this system?" to "Can you build something that works for real people in a real community?"

Students undertake their first **full-scope research project** — a year-long investigation that requires original data collection, vector-based analysis, cross-community collaboration, ethical review, and public presentation. They work in teams across schools in the network. They present to community members, not just teachers. The stakes are real. The audience is real. The consequences of bad data are real.

This is the year students stop being students of data and start being **practitioners**.

## Key Practices

### The Year-Long Project
Each student (or team of 2-3) selects a question that matters to their community. Examples from past cohorts:
- "Which dimensions of neighborhood walkability predict health outcomes in our zip code?"
- "How does the vector profile of successful community gardens differ from abandoned ones?"
- "Can we detect food insecurity from publicly available data without surveilling families?"

Projects require: a formal research proposal, ethical review by peers and teacher, original data collection, vector-based analysis, a written report, and a public presentation to community stakeholders.

### Cross-Community Data Collaboration
Students work with peers at partner schools to share data under governance agreements they negotiate themselves. They discover the real challenges of distributed research: inconsistent measurement, different cultural contexts, the politics of data interpretation. They learn to navigate disagreement about what the data means — a skill no amount of solo analysis teaches.

### Dimensionality Reduction Workshop
Students have worked with high-dimensional vectors for years. Now they learn the formal tools for making sense of them. PCA is introduced as "finding the directions in vector space where the data varies most" — students first do this visually with 2D point clouds, then extend to higher dimensions. t-SNE/UMAP concepts are introduced as "arranging high-dimensional data on a 2D map so similar things are near each other." These tools are connected to every Situation Room visualization they've ever seen.

### The Ethics Review Board
Student teams review each other's research proposals for ethical issues. Is the data collection consensual? Could the findings harm the population being studied? Are the conclusions supported by the data, or do they overreach? This peer review process mirrors real IRB review and teaches students that **rigor is not optional when your work affects people**.

|------------------|-------------------|

## Observable Growth

By June, an eleventh grader can:

- Design and execute a year-long research project with original data collection and vector-based analysis
- Navigate cross-school data collaboration including governance, methodology alignment, and interpretation disagreements
- Apply dimensionality reduction techniques and explain what they reveal and what they obscure
- Conduct ethical peer review of research proposals and receive critique of their own work
- Present findings to non-expert community stakeholders and respond to their questions
- Write a research report with methodology, limitations, and findings clearly distinguished
- Mentor younger students on independent research projects

## The Key Insight for This Year

When a student presents their community health research to the neighborhood council and a council member says "Your data shows our area is underserved, but you didn't talk to anyone who actually lives here" — and the student realizes the critique is valid, goes back, adds qualitative interviews to their vector analysis, and returns with a richer, more honest picture — they have learned the deepest lesson of data science: **numbers without context are just numbers**. The vector describes the structure. The story explains the meaning. You need both.

This is the full integration. The quipu-maker who encodes observations in string. The navigator who integrates multiple signals. The dimension-thinker who asks which measurements matter. The sovereignty advocate who asks who benefits. Now: the researcher who puts it all together and serves a community with honest, rigorous, human work. The eleventh grader is not just analyzing vectors. They are doing science — real science — with stakes, accountability, and an audience that cares about the answer.
