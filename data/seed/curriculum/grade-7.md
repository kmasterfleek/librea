# Grade 7: Everything Is Connected

**Theme**: Systems and Feedback
**Core Capacity**: Seeing how dimensions interact — feedback loops, correlations, and causal chains within vector data

---

## Philosophy

Seventh grade is the year students stop looking at dimensions in isolation and start seeing **systems**. A garden is not 8 independent measurements. Soil moisture affects pest count. Temperature affects bloom rate. Bloom rate affects pollinator visits. Pollinator visits affect fruit yield. Everything is connected, and the connections themselves carry information.

Students learn to trace these connections — first in their gardens, then in their communities, then in the data that describes both. The mathematical tools arrive naturally: correlation (do these two dimensions move together?), causal loops (does A cause B or does B cause A?), and feedback (does the output of the system change the input?). These are not abstract concepts. They are descriptions of what students observe every morning when they check their plots.

## Key Practices

### Connection Maps
Each student maintains a living diagram of their garden's internal relationships. Arrows connect dimensions: "soil moisture → plant height (positive)," "pest count → leaf health (negative)." Arrows are added only when students have evidence — at least three weeks of co-occurring change. By spring, the maps are dense and students debate which connections are causal vs. coincidental.

### The Correlation Exercise
Teacher projects a scatter plot: two dimensions of class data. "Is there a relationship?" Students identify positive, negative, or no correlation. Then the critical question: "Does this mean one causes the other?" The class debates. The teacher introduces confounding variables and hidden causes. Students learn that **correlation is visible in vector data; causation requires investigation**.

### Vector Search by Hand
Students receive a printed table of 20 vectors (5 dimensions each). They receive a query vector. Task: find the 3 most similar. First attempt: eyeball it. Second attempt: compute distances. Third attempt: "What if there were 10,000 vectors? You can't check them all." This motivates **indexing** — organizing vectors so you can search fast. The teacher introduces the idea without the algorithm: "What if similar vectors were stored near each other?"

### Community System Investigation
Each student selects a system in the school's surrounding community: the local food network, traffic patterns, the library's usage, a small business's customer flow. They define 5-8 dimensions, collect data over months, and map the feedback loops. Final presentations go to community members who participate in the system being studied.

## Observable Growth

By June, a seventh grader can:

- Maintain a connection map showing evidence-based relationships between dimensions
- Distinguish correlation from causation and explain the difference with examples
- Perform nearest-neighbor search on a small dataset by computing distances
- Explain why vector search needs indexing for large datasets
- Conduct a community system investigation with original data collection
- Draw a feedback loop diagram for a system they've studied
- Propose a Situation Room visualization and interpret the result

## The Key Insight for This Year

When a student traces a feedback loop in their community research — "the bus schedule determines who can shop at the market, which determines the market's revenue, which determines whether the market stays open, which determines whether the bus route is justified" — they have identified a **circular causal system** where no single variable is the root cause. They understand that the system itself has behavior that no individual dimension predicts.

This is the same insight that makes student vectors powerful: no single metric (GPA, attendance, SEL score) tells the story. The *relationships between dimensions* — the correlations, the feedback loops, the places where one dimension compensates for another — that's where the real information lives. A seventh grader who has mapped a community food system's feedback loops will not be surprised when told that "students with low GPA but high trajectory and high peer-connectedness often outperform predictions." They already know that dimensions interact.
